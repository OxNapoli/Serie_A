7   Methodology

This report uses a two-stage approach: (1) an Elo rating system that learns team strength from historical match results, and (2) a Monte Carlo simulation engine that uses those ratings to project the remainder of the season. Every number in this report flows from these two components.

7.1   Elo Rating System

What is Elo?

The Elo system was originally designed for chess by Arpad Elo. The core idea is simple: every team carries a single number — its rating — that represents its current strength. After every match, the winner gains rating points and the loser gives up the same number of points. The amount exchanged depends on how surprising the result was. A weak team beating a strong team triggers a large transfer; the expected favourite winning triggers a small one.

Starting Ratings

Every team begins with a rating of 1500. The model processes all available historical data (seasons 2020 onward) chronologically, so by the time we reach the current season each team has accumulated a rating that reflects several years of results.

The Expected-Result Formula

Before each match, the model computes the probability that the home team wins (ignoring draws for the moment). The home team’s effective rating is boosted by a home-field advantage of +30.7 Elo points to reflect the well-documented benefit of playing at home in Serie A.

The expected score for the home team is:

  Ehome = 1 / (1 + 10 ^ ((Raway − (Rhome + 30.7)) / 400))

Worked example. Suppose Napoli (rated 1580) hosts Lecce (rated 1420):

  Effective home rating = 1580 + 30.7 = 1610.7
  Rating gap            = 1420 − 1610.7 = -190.7
  Ehome                = 1 / (1 + 10 ^ (-190.7 / 400))
                        = 1 / (1 + 10 ^ (-0.4768))
                        ≈ 0.75

So the model gives Napoli roughly a 75% expected score in this matchup.

Actual Score

After the match is played the actual result is encoded as:

  • Home win: Shome = 1
  • Draw: Shome = 0.5
  • Away win: Shome = 0

Margin-of-Victory Multiplier

A 4–0 thrashing should update ratings more aggressively than a 1–0 scrape. The model applies a margin multiplier M that scales the update based on goal difference:

  M = clamp( ln(goal_difference + 1)1.61,  0.5,  3 )

If the match is a draw (goal difference = 0), M = 1.0. Examples:

The Rating Update

The change in the home team’s rating after a match is:

  Δ = K × M × (Shome − Ehome)

Where K = 18.28 controls the overall sensitivity of the system. The home team gains Δ points; the away team loses Δ points (zero-sum).

Continuing the Napoli–Lecce example. Suppose Napoli wins 3–1 (goal difference = 2):

  Shome = 1   (home win)
  Ehome ≈ 0.75
  M      = ln(3)1.61 ≈ 1.163
  Δ      = 18.28 × 1.163 × (1 − 0.75)
         ≈ 5.32

Napoli’s rating rises from 1580 to 1585.3; Lecce’s falls from 1420 to 1414.7. The update is modest because the result was expected — if Lecce had won 3–1, the rating transfer would have been much larger.

Season Reversion

Between seasons, every team’s rating is pulled 4.6% back toward 1500:

  Rnew season = Rend × 0.954 + 1500 × 0.046

This reflects the reality that squads change over the summer — transfers, managerial changes, player development — so last season’s final rating should not carry forward with full confidence. A team rated 1600 at the end of a season starts the next one at 1595.

7.2   Monte Carlo Simulation

Overview

Once we have Elo ratings for all teams, we want to answer questions like “What is the probability that Napoli wins the Scudetto?” or “How likely is Monza to be relegated?” There is no formula that directly gives these answers because the remaining fixtures interact — what happens in one match affects every subsequent match. The solution is Monte Carlo simulation: we play out the rest of the season thousands of times and count how often each outcome occurs.

This model runs 1e+05 simulations. Each simulation plays every remaining fixture independently using the Elo-derived probabilities. The result is not a single prediction but a full probability distribution over every possible final table.

Step 1: Set Up

For each of the 1e+05 parallel simulations:

  • Each team starts with its current actual points from matches already played.
  • Each team starts with its current Elo rating as computed by the historical engine above.

Step 2: Simulate Each Remaining Match

For every remaining fixture (there are 357 left in the current season), the model performs the following three sub-steps across all 1e+05 simulations simultaneously:

(a) Compute match probabilities. Using the two teams’ current simulation ratings (which may have drifted from their starting values as earlier simulated matches update them), the model computes the raw expected score Ehome exactly as described above. It then splits this into three separate probabilities — home win, draw, away win — using a draw model:

  Pdraw = pglobal × 1.23 × exp(-5.46 × (Ehome − 0.5)²)
  Phome = max(0,  Ehome − 0.5 × Pdraw)
  Paway = max(0,  (1 − Ehome) − 0.5 × Pdraw)

Where pglobal is the historical draw rate across all Serie A matches in the dataset. The draw probability peaks when the two teams are evenly matched (Ehome ≈ 0.5) and tapers off as the mismatch grows, which matches how football works — two mid-table sides draw far more often than a top side hosting a relegation candidate.

The three probabilities are then normalized so they sum to exactly 1.

Worked example. Suppose in a given simulation, Fiorentina (Elo 1530) hosts Juventus (Elo 1560) with a historical draw rate of 26%:

  Ehome  = 1 / (1 + 10 ^ ((1560 − (1530 + 30.7)) / 400))
         ≈ 0.501
  Pdraw  = 0.26 × 1.23 × exp(-5.46 × (0.501 − 0.5)²)
         ≈ 0.32

After normalization, a close match like this might produce something like Phome ≈ 40%, Pdraw ≈ 30%, Paway ≈ 30% — reflecting a genuine toss-up with a slight home edge.

(b) Randomly determine the result. A uniform random number is drawn for each simulation. If it falls below Phome the home team wins; if between Phome and Phome+Pdraw the match is a draw; otherwise the away team wins. Points are awarded: 3 for a win, 1 for a draw, 0 for a loss.

(c) Update simulation ratings (“hot” simulation). After the simulated result, the two teams’ Elo ratings within that simulation are updated using the same K-factor formula. Critically, a random chaos noise term is also added:

  Rnew = Rold + ΔElo + Normal(0, 25)

This noise (standard deviation = 25 Elo points) models the real-world unpredictability that a pure Elo system cannot capture: injuries, suspensions, managerial sackings, tactical changes, cup fatigue, and simple randomness. Without it, the model would be overconfident — assigning near-zero probabilities to upsets that actually happen regularly in Serie A. With it, weaker teams occasionally “get hot” and stronger teams occasionally slump, just as they do in reality.

Why “hot” simulation matters. Consider a model without rating updates. If Napoli’s rating is fixed at 1580 for all remaining matches, every match is independent. But in reality, a team on a winning streak is genuinely more dangerous than its pre-streak rating suggests. The hot simulation captures this: if Napoli wins three matches in a row within a simulation, their simulated rating rises, making them more likely to win the fourth. Conversely, a string of losses makes subsequent losses more likely. This produces wider, more realistic distributions.

Step 3: Aggregate Results

After all remaining matches have been simulated, we have 1e+05 complete final-season point totals for every team. We aggregate these to produce:

  • Expected points: The average final points across all simulations.
  • Title probability: The fraction of simulations in which a team finishes with the most points. Example: if Napoli finishes top in 45,000 out of 100,000 simulations, their title probability is 45%.
  • Top-4 probability: The fraction of simulations in which a team finishes in the top 4 (Champions League qualification).
  • Relegation probability: The fraction of simulations in which a team finishes in the bottom 3 (Serie A relegates three teams).
  • Points distribution: The full histogram of possible final points totals for each team, showing not just the average but the range of plausible outcomes.

Historical Evolution

To show how title and top-4 probabilities have changed over the course of the season, the model re-runs a smaller simulation (5,000 iterations) from the perspective of each past matchday. At matchday 10, for example, it takes the standings and Elo ratings as they were after matchday 10, simulates the remaining fixtures from that point, and records the resulting probabilities. Repeating this for every matchday produces the title race and Champions League evolution plots.

Strength of Schedule

The difficulty of each remaining fixture is measured as the opponent’s Elo rating, adjusted for home/away context. When a top-5 team plays at home, the opponent’s effective difficulty is reduced by 30.7 points; when the top-5 team plays away, the opponent’s effective difficulty is increased by 30.7 points. The average across all remaining fixtures gives each team’s overall schedule difficulty.

7.3   Key Assumptions & Limitations

  • No individual player modeling. The model treats each team as a single entity. It does not know about injuries, suspensions, transfers, or tactical changes. These effects enter only indirectly through the chaos noise term.
  • No goal-count simulation. Matches are simulated as win/draw/loss outcomes, not as scorelines. Tiebreakers based on goal difference or head-to-head records are not modeled — ties in points are broken arbitrarily within each simulation.
  • Home advantage is partly team-specific. Each team’s home-field bonus blends the global +30.7 Elo baseline with its own historical home performance (weight = 1), shrunk toward the global value for teams with limited history. It is not a full ground-by-ground model — travel distance, altitude, and crowd effects are not modeled directly.
  • Draw model is partly team-specific. The draw probability calibration blends the overall Serie A draw rate with each matchup’s two teams’ own historical draw rates (blend weight = 0.309), not a fully global rate.
  • No fixture congestion. The model does not account for midweek European matches, Coppa Italia, or compressed fixture schedules that may affect squad rotation and fatigue.
  • Chaos factor is empirically tuned. The noise term (25) was calibrated by comparing model predictions to actual historical outcomes. A higher value produces wider distributions (more uncertainty); a lower value produces narrower ones (more confidence in current ratings).
